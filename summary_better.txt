\subsection{Trajectory-based ("weather") optimization}

Trajectory-based optimization calibrates parameters \(\theta\) so that a simulated trajectory \(\mathbf{x}_\theta(t)\) stays close to a reference trajectory \(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{ref}}(t)\) over short horizons. In chaotic systems, this is fundamentally a \emph{local-in-time} problem: beyond the largest Lyapunov time, small parameter or state errors are amplified exponentially, and long-horizon matching (and its gradients) becomes dominated by chaotic drift rather than true parameter sensitivity.

\paragraph{Lyapunov-limited horizons.} Let \(\lambda\) be the largest Lyapunov exponent (for the classic Lorenz-63 parameters, \(\lambda \approx 0.9\), hence \(\tau_\lambda = 1/\lambda \approx 1.1\)). Gradients backpropagated through windows much longer than \(\tau_\lambda\) become unstable ("gradient blow-up"). Our strategy is therefore to restrict training to windows of physical length \(T_w = N_w\,\Delta t\) chosen such that \(T_w \lesssim \tau_\lambda\).

\paragraph{Windowed training with teacher forcing.} We partition the target trajectory into windows with starting indices \(\mathcal{S}\). For each start \(s\in\mathcal{S}\), we reinitialize the model state from the observation (teacher forcing), integrate forward for \(N_w\) steps of size \(\Delta t\), and compare model and target over this short horizon. A simple, effective objective is the window-averaged RMSE:
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{window}}(\theta) 
= \frac{1}{N_{\text{win}}} \sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}} \Bigg( \frac{1}{N_w} \sum_{i=1}^{N_w} \big\|\, \mathbf{x}_\theta(t_{s+i}) - \mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{ref}}(t_{s+i})\,\big\|^2 \Bigg)^{\!1/2}.
\]
Resetting at each window prevents long-term divergence from contaminating gradients inside a window and yields informative short-horizon sensitivities.

\paragraph{Implemented losses.} In addition to RMSE, we implemented several Enzyme-compatible losses to improve robustness:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{MAE} (mean absolute error): less sensitive to outliers than MSE.
	\item \textbf{Huber / pseudo-Huber}: quadratic for small residuals and linear for large ones; smooth and robust.
	\item \textbf{Probabilistic (Gaussian NLL)}: negative log-likelihood under fixed-variance Gaussian noise.
\end{itemize}
Each loss is available in a windowed form with a corresponding Enzyme-friendly scalar wrapper (e.g., \texttt{loss\_function\_enzyme}, \texttt{mae\_enzyme\_function}, \texttt{huber\_enzyme\_function}) that permits reverse-mode AD through the RK4 integrator.

\paragraph{Automatic differentiation with Enzyme.jl.} Gradients \(\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\theta\) are obtained by differentiating through the integration and loss using Enzyme.jl at the LLVM level. The project’s functions \texttt{compute\_gradients\_modular} and \texttt{compute\_gradients\_extended} select the appropriate enzyme call based on the loss, producing reverse-mode sensitivities without manual tangent/adjoint derivations.

\paragraph{Mini-batch windows and gradient averaging.} Training uses mini-batch SGD specialized to trajectories: each mini-batch consists of several windows (typically 32), gradients are computed per-window and averaged before a single parameter update. This averaging is essential in chaotic regimes: individual window gradients can be noisy due to sensitivity to initial conditions, but their mean reliably reflects parameter misfit. The training loop (\texttt{modular\_train!}) also supports window shuffling, a train/validation split by windows, and early stopping on validation loss.

\paragraph{Optimizers and regularization.} Updates are performed with Optimisers.jl (Adam, SGD, AdaGrad, or custom chains such as \texttt{ClipNorm}+Adam). Gradient clipping mitigates occasional large updates in windows approaching \(\tau_\lambda\). Empirically, stability depends more on \(T_w\) than on optimizer choice: when \(T_w \lesssim \tau_\lambda\), Adam/SGD/AdaGrad all converge; when \(T_w\) exceeds \(\sim\!2\,\tau_\lambda\), gradients become unreliable regardless of optimizer.

\paragraph{Practical settings used here.} In the experiments, \(\Delta t \approx 0.005\) (e.g., \(T=100\), \(M=20{,}000\)). Window sizes \(N_w\in[100, 400]\) (\(T_w\in[0.5,2.0]\)) were evaluated; best stability was observed for \(T_w\) near or below \(\tau_\lambda\). Mini-batch size is typically 32 windows; epochs \(\sim 200\). The update mask allows selecting which parameters to train (here: \(\sigma,\rho,\beta\)). Extended parameters (coordinate shifts \(x_s,y_s,z_s\) and \(\theta\)) are supported by the same machinery but are not central to this chapter.

\paragraph{Observed behavior.} With the above configuration, parameters are reliably recovered from synthetic data (e.g., improving an initial guess \(\rho=17\) toward \(\rho\approx 28\)). Convergence traces show smooth loss decay when \(T_w\) respects the Lyapunov constraint; gradient clipping provides additional safety near the stability boundary. Main failure modes are (i) \emph{overlong windows} (gradient blow-up), and (ii) \emph{phase misalignment} across windows (handled effectively by teacher forcing and robust losses).
